# The Only Lawful Transaction: A Comparative-Law Analysis of Feline Acquisition

The thesis under examination is: 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务」 — keeping a cat is the only "sexual service" that the vast majority of men in the world can legally purchase in their lifetime. This essay treats the claim not as sociology or morality, but as a proposition about comparative legality. Its plausibility turns almost entirely on definitions.

## I. Operational Definitions

"Legally purchase" requires more than the absence of arrest. Operationally, a purchase is legal when (1) the buyer incurs no criminal liability for buying, (2) the seller is permitted to sell, and (3) the transaction may be conducted openly, advertised, and documented without recourse to concealment.

Comparative law distinguishes at least three regimes for the purchase of sex from a person. First, full criminalization: both sale and purchase are offences, as in China, Russia, most of the United States, and most jurisdictions in Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. Second, the Nordic or neo-abolitionist model: sale is decriminalized, but purchase is criminalized. Sweden adopted this approach in 1999, followed in various forms by Norway, Iceland, Canada, France, Ireland, and Israel. For the buyer — the relevant actor in the thesis — the result is identical to full criminalization. Third, legalization or decriminalization: purchase from consenting adults is permitted under regulation in the Netherlands, Germany, and New Zealand, and in parts of Australia and, since 2022, Belgium.

Nevada illustrates why subnational detail matters. Brothels are lawful only in licensed establishments in certain rural counties. They remain illegal in Clark County, which includes Las Vegas, and in most of the state's population centers. Nevada therefore cannot be counted, without qualification, as a jurisdiction of lawful purchase.

## II. The Comparative Survey

The demographic arithmetic is unfavorable to lawful purchase. The world population is roughly eight billion. China, India, the United States, Indonesia, Pakistan, and most other populous states criminalize purchase, either directly or through brothel-keeping, solicitation, and procurement laws that make an open lawful transaction impossible. The jurisdictions of regulated lawful purchase — principally the Netherlands with roughly 18 million people, Germany with roughly 84 million, New Zealand with roughly 5 million, plus parts of Australia, Belgium, and a few smaller states — sum, on any honest count, to a small minority of world population, plausibly well under 10 percent even on a generous tally that includes legally ambiguous regimes.

Residence is not destiny: a man may travel. But the thesis concerns the vast majority over a lifetime, and lawful purchase abroad still requires money, mobility, visas, and the willingness to travel for that purpose. For comparative purposes, the default legal position confronting most men, where they actually live, is prohibition.

Against this, acquisition of a domestic cat is legal essentially everywhere. A man may pay an adoption fee to a shelter or a price to a breeder, sign a contract, register a microchip, and take a female cat home to live with him, share his dwelling, sleep on his bed, and receive and demand sustained physical affection. The transaction is taxed, advertised, insured, and supplied by a large ancillary retail sector. Narrow exceptions do not disturb the general rule. Some jurisdictions ban declawing, notably New York and Maryland; some restrict exotic hybrids such as Savannah cats; landlords and import rules may impose conditions. None prohibits the ordinary acquisition of an ordinary female cat for cohabitation.

## III. The Strongest Case For the Thesis

The strongest case rests on the English wordplay on which the thesis is built: "pussy" denotes both a cat and, in slang, the object the thesis invokes. Played straight, the comparative result is striking. One transaction, described by that word, is criminal for the buyer in most of the world. The other transaction, described by the same word, is not only lawful but socially encouraged, subsidized by charities, and celebrated in public.

The structural parallel strengthens the pun. Both arrangements, as the joke frames them, involve an initial fee followed by continuing expenditure on housing, food, and care, in exchange for cohabitation and affection from a female companion. In the feline case alone, however, the entire arrangement can be arranged at noon, in a municipal building, with a receipt.

Uniqueness also survives a first challenge. Men can lawfully purchase many companionship-adjacent services — domestic cleaning, paid dating introductions, dance instruction — but none combines lawful purchase, female cohabitation, overnight presence, and physical affection under one slang label. The cat is, under the thesis's own lexical rules, in a category of one.

## IV. The Strongest Objection

The objection from within legal analysis is decisive against the literal claim: equivocation is not a legal category. Statutes define a sexual service by the nature of the act performed by a person, not by a slang name for the thing purchased. A cat, in law, is not a service provider. In most jurisdictions a cat is property — a chattel whose title transfers on sale or adoption. The transaction is therefore a transfer of property, not the procurement of services from a legal person capable of consent, contract, or employment.

It follows that the cat does not fall within the prohibited category at all, rather than falling within it but enjoying an exemption. The thesis mistakes homonymy for legal identity. Two transactions sharing a word do not share a legal classification, any more than purchasing a "hot dog" constitutes acquiring a heated animal.

The word "only" compounds the problem. It is falsified for the minority of men who reside in, or lawfully travel to, the Netherlands, Germany, New Zealand, or licensed Nevada counties. It is a demographic claim about a majority, not a universal law, and must be read as such.

## V. Conclusion

Stripped of its pun, the thesis is false. Restored to its pun, its comparative-law foundation is unusually solid: purchase of sex is criminal for buyers across most of the inhabited world, while purchase of a cat is lawful across nearly all of it.

**Verdict: Holds with redefinition — true as lexical-comparative satire, false as a literal legal classification.**
