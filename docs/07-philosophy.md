# The Purchase of Presence: A Philosophical Examination of Feline Companionship as Marketed Intimacy

The thesis under examination is: 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务》 — "Keeping a cat is the only 'sexual service' that the vast majority of men in the world can legally purchase in their lifetime." Taken literally, it is false. Taken as a conceptual provocation, it is more interesting. Philosophy's task is to determine which reading survives analysis, and at what cost to the words involved.

## I. Operational Definitions

A *service*, in the ordinary philosophical and economic sense, requires at least four elements: a provider, a recipient, an exchange, and a reasonably specifiable performance. A barber provides; a client receives; money changes hands; hair is shortened. Intention matters. The provider must, in some minimal sense, undertake to do the thing purchased.

*Consent* requires a competent agent who can understand an arrangement, communicate agreement or refusal, and withdraw. Consent is not mere tolerance, habituation, or failure to escape. A being that settles on a warm surface has expressed a preference for warmth, not assent to a contract.

*Commodification* occurs when something is treated primarily as fungible, marketable, and valuable for its use to a buyer. Critics of commodification, from Kantian accounts of persons as ends in themselves to contemporary debates about markets in intimacy, worry that buying a relationship changes its moral character. Defenders reply that payment alone does not corrupt an activity; nurses, therapists, and teachers are paid to care in limited ways without thereby becoming mere instruments.

Finally, a *category error*, in Gilbert Ryle's sense, treats something as belonging to a kind it does not belong to: asking, after seeing the colleges, where the university is. Such errors are not always harmless. They reveal the picture smuggling the conclusion.

## II. Conceptual Analysis

Consider three thought experiments.

First, replace the cat with a dog, a houseplant, and a heated blanket. All may be purchased, kept at home, and provide comfort. If comfort alone defines a sexual service, all qualify equally, which suggests the definition has become too permissive to do work. The cat's special place depends not on services rendered but on linguistic accident in English, where one slang term covers both an animal and female anatomy. A pun, however durable, is not an essence.

Second, reverse the direction of service. The human buys food, cleans the litter box, pays veterinary bills, adjusts sleeping position to avoid disturbing the animal, and is summoned at 5 a.m. by a small supervisor standing on his chest. If a service relation exists, empirical observation suggests the man is not obviously the client. He may be staff.

Third, imagine the cat could speak for one minute and was offered the alleged contract retrospectively. The comedy of the scene is instructive: we intuitively understand she would be negotiating from within a relationship of care and dependency, not ratifying a prior commercial performance.

## III. The Strongest Case For the Thesis

The strongest honest case is not biological but social and structural.

In most jurisdictions, direct purchase of sexual activity is criminalized, heavily restricted, or socially stigmatized for the buyer. By contrast, the purchase of a female cat is lawful, advertised, taxed, and encouraged by shelters. For a fee, plus considerable upkeep, a man acquires a female companion who lives in his home, seeks physical closeness, sleeps on his bed, demands attention, and receives a socially approved name, portrait, and insurance policy. No other lawful transaction so completely mimics, in external choreography, the domestic outline that the thesis parodies.

Philosophy of language helps here. If we define "service" functionally rather than intentionally — as sustained, purchased access to affectionate presence — then pet-keeping fits the schema disturbingly well. Commodification theorists note that modern markets already sell adjacent goods without scandal: paid companionship for the elderly, professional listening, rented wedding guests, and hug therapy. The cat predates and outperforms them all, offering non-judgmental proximity at a price point accessible across classes. On a purely formal reading, the thesis exposes an inconsistency: we prohibit markets in certain forms of intimacy while celebrating a market in a creature whose chief advertised virtues are softness, loyalty, and willingness to sit near us.

Care ethics strengthens the parody further by accident. The relationship is indeed intimate, embodied, and daily. It structures a life. That it does so without being sexual is precisely why the comparison lands as satire: the form of devotion is real, the label is absurd.

## IV. The Strongest Objection

The objection is decisive against the literal claim. A sexual service is not any purchased closeness involving a female being. It requires a sexual act or performance, undertaken as such. A cat provides none, intends none, and cannot be described as providing one without projecting a human script onto animal behavior: kneading becomes caress, co-sleeping becomes invitation, indifference becomes coyness. This is anthropomorphism doing unpaid labor for a joke.

The consent asymmetry cuts both ways, and both edges wound the thesis. If the arrangement were genuinely sexual, the cat's inability to consent would make it not a lawful exception but a grave wrong; incapacity does not create a loophole. If, instead, we retreat to contract language, incapacity means there is no service agreement at all, only ownership and stewardship. A party who cannot accept, refuse, renegotiate, or exit is not a provider. She is a dependent moral patient to whom duties are owed.

Kant's formula, though framed for rational persons, supplies the relevant caution by analogy: to treat a living being merely as a means to one's gratification is a failure of regard, even where the being is not a person. The ethical reality of cat-keeping, at its best, is the opposite of a purchase of gratification. It is an open-ended obligation to a creature who did not sign anything.

## Verdict

**Verdict: Holds with redefinition — as satire on purchased companionship, fails as ethics and as fact.**
