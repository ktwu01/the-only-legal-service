# Purchased Closeness: An Evolutionary-Psychological Assessment of Feline Companionship

The thesis under examination is: 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务」. Translated literally, it claims that keeping a cat is the only sexual service most men can legally purchase in their lifetime. Evolutionary psychology cannot evaluate a pun directly. It can, however, evaluate the psychological systems the pun exploits: attachment, caregiving, tactile reward, and anthropomorphism. When those systems are defined precisely, the thesis proves less absurd than its wording suggests, though not in the sense its wording implies.

## 1. Operational Definitions

For present purposes, *attachment* is a durable affective bond characterized by proximity-seeking, distress at separation, and use of the other as a source of comfort. In pet research it is measured with self-report instruments such as the Lexington Attachment to Pets Scale, on which many owners score their animals comparably to close human relationships.

*Tactile comfort* is the stress-reducing effect of gentle, affiliative touch, mediated in part by slow-conducting C-tactile afferents and by changes in autonomic arousal. *Anthropomorphism* is the attribution of humanlike intentions, emotions, gender, and social roles to a nonhuman animal. *Being needed* is the perception that one's care is necessary to another's welfare, which activates the caregiving behavioral system described by Bowlby and later researchers. Finally, *sexual* is defined narrowly, in motivational terms, as pertaining to the mating system, distinct from both the attachment and caregiving systems. This distinction will become decisive.

## 2. Examination by the Methods of the Discipline

Evolutionary psychology assumes that humans possess several partly independent motivational systems shaped by different adaptive problems: mating, pair-bonding, parenting, affiliation, and status. A single relationship can activate several systems at once, which is why category errors are so common in everyday language.

The domestic cat appears unusually well designed, by accident rather than intention, to activate at least three non-mating systems simultaneously. First, cats exhibit what Konrad Lorenz called baby schema: relatively large eyes, a rounded face, a small nose, and a high forehead. Across experiments, such features reliably increase attention, perceived cuteness, and self-reported caregiving motivation in adults. Second, cats solicit care in ways that resemble contingent social exchange. They approach, vocalize, rub, knead, and withdraw. The unpredictability is important. Intermittent social reward sustains engagement more effectively than constant availability, a principle familiar from basic reinforcement research.

Third, contact itself has measurable physiological correlates. Studies of human-animal interaction, varying in quality and size, commonly find that petting a familiar animal is associated with modest reductions in cortisol and blood pressure, and in some settings with increased oxytocin. These effects are neither large nor unique to cats, but they are real enough to explain why owners describe the experience in bodily, rather than merely sentimental, terms.

Fourth, co-sleeping is normative rather than deviant. Surveys of cat owners in several Western countries regularly find that roughly half or more allow the cat on the bed, and a substantial minority report sleeping in direct contact with it. Psychologically, the bed is a high-trust location associated with vulnerability, warmth, and pair-bonding. Admission to it therefore carries symbolic weight disproportionate to the cat's intentions, which are presumably thermal.

## 3. The Strongest Honest Case For the Thesis

The strongest case begins with a constraint on adult men. In many societies, adult male access to non-sexual physical affection is unusually restricted. Touch between male friends is limited by norm, touch from female friends is frequently interpreted through a mating frame, and professional touch services, such as massage, are time-limited, rule-bound, and explicitly non-domestic. A man may therefore experience what researchers call touch hunger or affection deprivation despite having an otherwise functional social life.

A cat resolves this constraint legally, continuously, and at low social cost. For an adoption fee and continuing expenditure on food, litter, and veterinary care, the owner obtains a living companion who resides in his home, seeks proximity on a daily basis, permits and solicits stroking, sleeps beside him, and depends on him for survival. If the cat is female, and if the owner anthropomorphizes her using conventional feminine stereotypes of softness, capriciousness, and affectionate demand, then the purchased package closely resembles the *affiliative surface* of an intimate relationship: cohabitation, bodily closeness, bed-sharing, jealousy of attention, and the gratifying experience of being needed.

From an evolutionary perspective, the arrangement is efficient precisely because it bypasses negotiation. Human intimacy requires mutual consent, reciprocal obligation, and tolerance of rejection. A cat's consent is limited and frequently withdrawn by walking away, which owners tend to interpret as temperament rather than personal failure. The caregiving system is thus rewarded without exposing the owner to the full reputational and emotional risks of the human mating market. If "sexual service" is redefined, very loosely, as commercially obtained access to embodied affection from a female-coded companion, the thesis describes a genuine and widespread transaction.

## 4. The Strongest Objection From Within Psychology

The objection is that the redefinition commits a classic systems error. Attachment, caregiving, and mating are dissociable. Hazan and Shaver's extension of attachment theory to adult romance, and subsequent work on behavioral systems, treats sexual desire, emotional bonding, and care provision as separable components that normally cooperate but can occur independently. A patient may love his mother, care for his child, desire a partner, and cuddle a cat without any one relationship substituting fully for the others.

The evidence supports that separation. Pet attachment correlates with reduced loneliness for some owners, but it does not eliminate interest in human partnership, nor do strongly attached owners typically report that the animal fulfills romantic needs. Indeed, heavy anthropomorphism can coexist with acute human loneliness, suggesting the systems are being fed unequally rather than satisfied as a whole. Furthermore, the cat's presumed femininity is projected, not purchased. Owners anthropomorphize male cats in similar terms, name both sexes as family members, and describe both as children, which indicates that the caregiving system, not the mating system, is doing most of the work.

In short, the thesis confuses the reward overlap between systems with identity between them. Stroking, warmth, and co-sleeping are shared outputs. They are not proof of a shared function.

## 5. Verdict

**Verdict: Holds with redefinition — as purchased affiliative and tactile companionship, not as a sexual service in any psychologically meaningful sense.**
