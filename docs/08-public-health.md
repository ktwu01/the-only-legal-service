# A Harm-Reduction Intervention Disguised as a Pet: A Public Health Assessment

The thesis under review — 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务」 — depends on an English pun and a substitution claim. Treated literally, it is false. Treated as a population-health proposition about legally purchased, co-resident female companionship involving physical affection, it becomes testable. This essay tests it.

## Operational Definitions

**Keeping a cat** is defined here as the legal acquisition and household maintenance of a domestic cat, most often female if the pun is to survive, with ongoing exposure through cohabitation, handling, lap contact, shared sleeping surfaces, and litter or feeding duties.

**"Sexual service,"** for public health purposes, cannot be retained in its literal sense. No sexual activity occurs in the feline intervention, and none is implied clinically. The redefined exposure is therefore: paid access to sustained, affectionate, tactile companionship within the home. Outcomes of interest are loneliness, stress, sleep, cardiovascular risk, injury, and infection.

**The literal comparator** is commercial sexual activity, legal in few jurisdictions, illegal or semi-legal in most, and associated in the literature with sexually transmitted infections, violence, exploitation, and barriers to care. The comparison is therefore not between two versions of the same act, but between a prohibited high-risk activity and a legal low-risk substitute that partially overlaps in one outcome domain: companionship.

**Legally purchase** means a transaction that can be advertised, taxed, regulated, and taken to a veterinarian, a landlord, or small-claims court without concealment — a property that itself improves health surveillance.

## Comparative Risk Assessment

Public health evaluates interventions by absolute risk, severity, and distribution, not by moral category.

On the benefit side, evidence for pet ownership is modest and inconsistent. Some observational studies associate cat ownership with lower stress reactivity and reduced loneliness, particularly among people living alone. Others find no effect. Cardiovascular findings are famously mixed: a widely cited analysis of survey data once suggested lower cardiovascular mortality among cat owners, while subsequent cohorts have found null or even adverse associations. No randomized trial can credibly assign cats for decades, so residual confounding remains irreducible. Healthier, wealthier, and more stably housed people are more likely to acquire and keep pets; the cat may be a marker of health rather than a cause of it.

On the harm side, the feline exposure is not zero-risk, but its profile differs in kind from the comparator. There is no route for HIV, syphilis, gonorrhea, or chlamydia transmission from a cat to a human through normal companionship. Physical affection in this model does not require barrier protection, post-exposure prophylaxis, or partner notification. From a harm-reduction standpoint, this is complete risk elimination for that category, achieved not by safer performance of the same act but by substitution of activity.

Other risks replace them, at lower incidence and usually lower severity. These include allergic disease, bite and scratch infections, and a small set of zoonoses discussed below. At population level, the burden is diffuse and largely manageable with veterinary care, hand hygiene, and litter precautions, rather than concentrated in criminalized networks with poor access to services.

## The Strongest Case For the Thesis

The strongest honest case is a harm-reduction and access case.

First, access: for the vast majority of men worldwide, the literal service is either illegal, unaffordable on a sustained basis, socially sanctioned, or all three. A cat, by contrast, is purchasable in almost every jurisdiction for a modest adoption fee, resides legally in the home, and provides companionship continuously rather than by the hour. No other legal market offers this combination of co-residence, tactile affection, and permanence.

Second, substitution: public health has long accepted imperfect substitutes when they displace riskier behavior — nicotine replacement, supervised consumption, needle exchange. If part of the demand in the illegal market is for intimacy, presence, and being wanted by another living being who greets one at the door and demands food at 5 a.m., then the cat satisfies that component while delivering none of the STI or violence risk. The satisfaction is partial. So is that of a nicotine patch. Partial substitution at population scale still prevents cases.

Third, ancillary benefits: routine care for another organism structures the day, prompts movement, and may buffer depressive symptoms, though effect sizes are small and causation uncertain. Even a null average effect would leave the intervention preferable on safety grounds alone if companionship is the outcome sought.

On these restricted terms — legal access, companionship outcome, risk displacement — the thesis performs surprisingly well.

## The Strongest Objection

Medicine must object on two grounds: the cat is not harmless, and the evidence is not causal.

Allergy alone affects a substantial minority — roughly 10 to 20 percent of adults are sensitized to cats — and can exacerbate asthma. Scratches can transmit *Bartonella henselae*, the agent of cat-scratch disease; bites, though less common from cats than dogs proportionally severe, can inoculate oral flora deep into a puncture wound and require urgent care. Dermatophytosis (ringworm) is transmissible and tedious. Toxoplasmosis requires nuance: infection with *Toxoplasma gondii* is common worldwide, often estimated at around a third of humanity, but undercooked meat and environmental exposure are major routes; indoor cats fed commercial food are a minor source, though litter handling in pregnancy still warrants precaution. Tripping over a cat is also a non-trivial fall mechanism in older adults, and nocturnal activity can fragment sleep — an awkward outcome for an intervention marketed, in the thesis, partly on sharing a bed.

More fundamentally, correlation is not causation. Pet-health studies suffer from selection bias, publication bias toward charming results, and exposure misclassification: "cat owner" covers both a person bonded to an indoor animal and a person whose barn cat they see monthly. Until effect estimates survive adjustment for income, housing, baseline health, and social support, public health cannot prescribe cats, only decline to prohibit them.

Finally, the redefinition does all the work. Once "sexual service" is redefined as "affectionate companionship," the thesis no longer describes sex, services, or risk as clinicians use those terms. The category error is the joke, but also the limitation.

## Verdict

**Verdict: Holds with redefinition — as a legal, low-risk substitute for companionship, not as a sexual service.**
