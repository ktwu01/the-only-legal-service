# The Only Legal Purchase: An Economic Analysis of Feline Acquisition as a Market for Companionship

The thesis under examination is: 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务」. Translated literally, it claims that keeping a cat is the only sexual service most men can legally buy in their lifetime. Economics cannot evaluate wordplay directly. It can, however, evaluate markets, prices, and legal constraints on exchange, which is where the claim becomes testable.

### Definitions

A purchasable service is defined here as a flow of benefits obtained through voluntary exchange, where a buyer pays consideration and a seller, or a lawful transfer mechanism, grants access under enforceable terms. It is distinct from the purchase of an asset. An asset is acquired once and then yields a flow of services over time, at additional cost.

Price therefore has two components. The acquisition price is the upfront fee. The total cost of ownership includes recurring expenditures on food, litter, veterinary care, and housing compliance, plus opportunity costs in time and space.

A market exists when buyers and sellers can transact openly, advertise, set prices, and rely on legal enforcement. Where a transaction is criminalized, the formal market is missing, even if an informal or illegal market persists at higher risk and transaction cost.

Companionship, for present purposes, is a bundle of characteristics: co-residence, physical proximity, tactile contact such as cuddling, sleeping in the same bed, and reciprocal demands for attention. Following the characteristics approach to demand, consumers do not purchase goods for their labels, but for the characteristics they deliver.

### Examination

Measured by these definitions, the literal service named in the thesis has no legal formal market in most jurisdictions. In the United States, commercial sex is illegal in almost all states, with a narrow exception in certain licensed counties in Nevada. Similar prohibitions, or heavily restricted regimes, prevail in much of the world. The economic consequence is not absence of demand, but absence of a lawful price, standard contract, consumer protection, or open advertising.

The market for cats, by contrast, is large, formal, and highly legible. Industry estimates place United States pet expenditure on the order of $150 billion per year in the mid-2020s, with the global pet industry considerably larger. Adoption fees for a shelter cat commonly run roughly $50 to $200, depending on location, age, and whether spaying, vaccination, and microchipping are included. Breeder prices are higher, often several hundred to a few thousand dollars.

Acquisition is only the entry fee. Annual ownership costs are commonly estimated at roughly $300 to $1,000 or more, excluding major veterinary emergencies. Over a typical lifespan of 12 to 18 years, lifetime expenditure comfortably reaches five figures. Structurally, the cat is therefore not a one-time purchase. It resembles a hybrid model: a low, subsidized upfront price, often below cost at shelters, followed by a mandatory, self-administered subscription in food, litter, and care. Unlike a digital subscription, cancellation is heavily penalized by moral, social, and in many places legal obligations of animal welfare.

Demand-side evidence is consistent with a high willingness to pay for companionship itself. Consumers separately purchase dating-app subscriptions, social clubs, and therapy, all of which sell, in part, structured social contact. The pet market isolates this willingness more cleanly: a substantial share of owners cite companionship as a primary motive, and continue paying recurring costs long after any utilitarian function such as pest control has become negligible in an urban apartment.

### The Strongest Case For the Thesis

Redefined in characteristics terms, the case is strong. For a modest acquisition fee and predictable upkeep, a man lawfully obtains what no other formal market will sell him as a package: a female, in roughly half of adoptions, co-resident companion who is permitted, indeed encouraged, to share domestic space, seek physical closeness, sleep on the bed, and make persistent, socially approved demands for affection.

Crucially, the transaction is fully legible to banks, landlords subject to pet agreements, tax authorities, and family members. Payment can be made by card. Receipts are issued. No other purchase delivering this specific bundle of co-residence and tactile companionship combines such low legal risk, low social stigma, and low search cost.

The pun on "pussy" performs the economic identification. It asserts that two goods share a name and, the argument continues, overlap in delivered characteristics sufficiently that the cheaper, legal good becomes the only effective market outlet for demand that would otherwise face prohibition. On that basis, the cat is not merely a pet. It is the sole legal clearing mechanism for an otherwise missing market.

### The Strongest Objection From Economics

The objection is one of classification, and it is decisive against the literal claim. What is purchased is not the service named in the thesis, but an animal and the subsequent flow of companionship characteristics it provides. Economic goods are defined by their characteristics and by the contract actually enforced. No seller of a cat offers, warrants, or is legally liable for the service implied by the pun. To count the transaction as a purchase of that service commits an equivocation fallacy: identical labels are treated as identical commodities.

A second problem follows. If any legal purchase delivering affectionate co-residence counts, the cat is not unique. Dogs deliver a closely overlapping bundle, often at higher price and higher time cost, and are equally legal. The thesis therefore fails on exclusivity unless it arbitrarily privileges the linguistic coincidence over economic substitutability. Once substitutes are admitted, the cat is the cheapest and lowest-maintenance entrant in a broader companionship market, not its sole provider.

Finally, ownership involves negative externalities and obligations absent from a standard service contract: the provider cannot consent, cannot exit, and must be maintained regardless of the buyer's satisfaction. This makes the model closer to adoption of a dependent than to purchase of a service, a distinction economics preserves precisely to avoid mispricing welfare obligations as consumption.

### Verdict

**Verdict: holds with redefinition — as the cheapest legal market for co-resident affectionate companionship, not as the literal service named.**
