# Legal Companionship and the Cat Dad: A Sociological Assessment

The thesis under examination is: 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务」 ("Keeping a cat is the only 'sexual service' that the vast majority of men in the world can legally purchase in their lifetime"). The argument depends on the English double meaning of "pussy" and on a structural resemblance: for an acquisition fee plus continuing upkeep, a man obtains a female companion who resides in his household, seeks physical closeness, sleeps in his bed, and is licensed to demand affection at inconvenient hours. Sociology cannot evaluate the pun. It can, however, evaluate the social arrangement the pun describes.

## Operational Definitions

*Companionship* is treated here as a social good: the reliable presence of another being whose proximity reduces loneliness and around whom daily routines are organised. It is distinct from both kinship and friendship, though it borrows the rituals of each. The *household* is the unit of co-residence, shared space, and shared expenditure, regardless of the species of its members. *Stigma* is the social penalty attached to a disclosed identity or transaction, measurable in concealment behaviour: what people admit to on a dating profile, at work, or to their family, and what they do not. A *human-animal bond* counts as a social relationship, for present purposes, if it is durable, named, reciprocal in behaviour if not in language, and mourned when it ends. By these definitions, a cat qualifies as a companion, a household member, and a relationship partner in every respect except the one the thesis is joking about.

## Examination

Three background trends make the claim sociologically legible rather than merely rude. First, solo living has normalised. In many Western countries, roughly a quarter to a third of households are now one-person households, and in some large cities the share is higher still. A substantial population of men therefore manages the problem of an empty dwelling as a permanent condition, not an interlude. Second, pets have been reclassified upward. In pet-owner surveys, large majorities, often around four-fifths or more, describe their animals as family members, and veterinary spending, pet insurance, and birthday observances have followed the reclassification. Third, and most decisively, the stigma gradient runs in opposite directions for the two transactions being compared. Purchasing the literal service is, in most jurisdictions, illegal, and where it is tolerated it remains heavily stigmatised: it is concealed from employers, partners, and kin, and disclosure carries reputational cost. Cat ownership carries no such penalty. "Cat dad" is printed on merchandise, declared in social media biographies, and deployed on dating profiles as a positive signal of domestic competence and emotional availability. One purchase must be hidden to preserve status. The other raises it.

The market structure reinforces the point. The cat is acquired through a fully licit, indeed charitable, institutional channel: shelters, breeders, and rescue organisations advertise openly, charge an adoption fee, and in many cases interview the purchaser to assess his suitability as a provider. No other arrangement in which an adult male pays to install a female companion in his home is preceded by a home check intended to protect the companion.

## The Strongest Case For

Argued in good faith, the thesis identifies a real institutional monopoly. For the majority of men, every other route to guaranteed co-resident female companionship is either not purchasable (marriage and partnership are, at least formally, non-market and mutual), not co-resident (friendship, however warm, goes home at night), or not legal (the literal referent). The cat alone is available on demand, for money, without negotiation with the companion's family, employer, or schedule, and with the state's full approval. The resulting package matches the thesis point by point: cohabitation, tactile affection on the companion's initiative, shared sleeping arrangements, vocal demands for attention, and a maintenance obligation, in food, litter, and veterinary care, that continues for the companion's lifetime and is socially enforced. A man who neglected these payments would be judged more harshly by his peers than a man who simply stopped calling a human acquaintance. Sociology should also note the direction of emotional labour: the owner performs feeding, cleaning, and reassurance, and receives presence and purring in return. That this exchange is openly described as love, while formally similar paid exchanges are described as vice, is a finding about labelling, not about the exchanges themselves.

## The Strongest Objection

Sociology also supplies the refutation. Social relationships, in the discipline's stricter usage, require mutual recognition between persons who can, in principle, refuse, renegotiate, or exit. A cat can do the first of these at the level of behaviour, and famously does, but it cannot consent to the arrangement, hold rights within it, or be a party to a service contract. Legally the cat is property; sociologically it is a family member; it is at no point a service provider. The very absence of stigma, which the case for the thesis treats as evidence, is on this reading the counter-evidence: societies do not stigmatise cat ownership precisely because they do not classify it with the purchase of sex, and classification, for sociology, is not a decorative error but the phenomenon itself. The pun works in English and in translation. The institutions do not coincide in any language. It should also be recorded that the claim is empirically overbroad: women keep cats at comparable rates under the same terms, which suggests the good being purchased is companionship as such, with the gendered framing supplied by the joke rather than by the data.

## Verdict

**Verdict: Holds with redefinition — true as a claim about legally purchasable, stigma-free companionship, false as a claim about sex.**
