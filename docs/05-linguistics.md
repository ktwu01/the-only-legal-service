# The Cat in the Lexicon: A Semantic Analysis of a Purchased Companion

The thesis under examination is: 「养猫是世界上绝大多数男性一生中唯一能够合法购买的性服务」. Translated literally, it asserts that keeping a cat is the only sexual service most men in the world can legally purchase in their lifetime. A semantic analysis does not ask whether the sentence is amusing. It asks a prior question: under which assignments of meaning to its words, if any, is the sentence true?

## Operational Definitions

Four terms require fixing. First, *polysemy*: a single word form carrying multiple related senses, as distinct from accidental homonymy. Second, *slang sense*: a sense that is register-restricted and socially marked, but nevertheless conventional for competent speakers. Third, *metonymy*: reference by association, whereby a word for one entity stands for a related entity. Fourth, *service*: in its ordinary commercial sense, an activity performed for a beneficiary in exchange for payment, as opposed to the sale of a physical good.

For the thesis to be true without redefinition, three conditions must hold simultaneously. The English word *pussy* must denote both the animal and the object of the alleged service in the same utterance, without equivocation. The Chinese expression for "sexual service" (*xìng fúwù*, literally sex-related service) must cover the keeping of a cat. And *purchase* must apply in the same sense to adoption fees and upkeep as it does to a commercial transaction. If any condition requires switching senses mid-sentence, the result is not one false argument; it is, more precisely, not one argument at all.

## Lexical Analysis

The word *puss*, and its diminutive *pussy*, have denoted a cat in English since roughly the sixteenth century, plausibly with cognates in Dutch, German, and Scandinavian languages for calling or naming a cat. For centuries this feline sense was unremarkable, appearing in nursery language and in affectionate address to cats and, by extension, to girls and women.

The slang sense, denoting female anatomy and, by metonymy, a woman in sexual terms, is attested much later, roughly from the nineteenth century into widespread twentieth-century use. It may be an extension from softness, fur, and affectionate naming, or a partly independent development. What is not disputed is the chronological order. The cat sense is historically prior; the slang sense is secondary to a word already domesticated.

This ordering matters. The joke does not coin a new meaning. It reactivates an old one and forces it to collide with a newer, taboo one. The hearer must hold both senses active at once, which is precisely the processing condition for a pun, and precisely the condition excluded from a literal assertion.

Translation exposes the mechanism. Chinese *xìng fúwù* has no feline sense at all. Its components are transparent: sex, plus service. A Chinese rendering therefore states the claim baldly and produces bafflement, not recognition, unless the reader imports the English polysemy as background knowledge. The thesis survives translation only partially: its propositional clothing crosses languages, but its semantic engine does not. Parallels exist elsewhere, though imperfectly. French *chatte*, for example, is both a female cat and a vulgar slang term, offering a closer structural analogue, whereas many languages keep the two domains lexically separate. The distribution is contingent, not universal, which already limits any claim phrased about "the world."

## The Strongest Case For

The honest case for the thesis, within semantics, is that slang senses are real senses. A descriptivist cannot dismiss the slang meaning of *pussy* as "not what the word means," because millions of speakers reliably use and understand it. Nor can one dismiss metonymy, a normal engine of language: words routinely extend from animal to person and from part to whole. English pet names for partners — kitten, tiger, honey — document a productive overlap between the animal and affectionate lexicons.

On this generous reading, the thesis is a compressed observation about lexical history: the same word came to cover a legally purchasable household companion and a domain of transaction that, in most jurisdictions, is heavily restricted. If truth is allowed at the level of wordplay — truth about the lexicon, if not about the world — the sentence correctly reports that English permits this collision, and that the law permits one referent and largely restricts the other.

## The Strongest Objection

Linguistics also owns the fatal objection: equivocation. A pun requires two senses; a proposition permits one. In "a man legally purchases a pussy," the sentence is true under the feline sense and unsubstantiated under the slang sense; no single reading makes the full thesis, including its "sexual service" predicate, true of the same referent. The cat is not a service, the companion is not the anatomy, and the woman implied by the metonymy is not the animal on the bed. Each step substitutes a neighbour in the semantic network for the previous node, producing the sensation of an argument while traversing, in fact, a chain of associations.

Remove equivocation and the thesis reduces to the true but trivial claim that men may legally buy cats. Preserve it, and no stable proposition remains for law, economics, or sociology to evaluate.

## Verdict

**Verdict: Holds with redefinition — true only as a statement about English polysemy, false as a statement about services.**
